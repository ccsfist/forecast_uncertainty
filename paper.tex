\abstract{As of 2022, nearly 5 million smallholder farmers across more than a dozen countries are covered by Anticipatory Action (AA) programs. AA programs are aid schemes triggered when seasonal forecasts of precipitation fall below a locally-determined drought threshold. These thresholds are generally calculated to meet a donor- or government-specified expected trigger frequency. However, the observational precipitation record in many regions with AA programs is limited, and interannual and interdecadal autocorrelation in the climate patterns driving seasonal precipitation further reduce the effective sample size used to calculate these thresholds. AA trigger thresholds are highly sensitive to this limited observational record, increasing the risk that action is not triggered when it is needed or that programs become financially unsustainable due to excessive triggers. In practice, forecasters are aware of this uncertainty and ad hoc decisions on whether to trigger action are often made if seasonal forecasts are close to the threshold. Seeking a more formalized, statistically sound alternative to these ad hoc approaches, governments and humanitarian agencies have asked for transparent and intuitive metrics of trigger uncertainty. To address this demand, we present a method for quantifying the uncertainty in drought thresholds due to a limited observational record using bootstrap resampling. Examining United Nations operational AA forecasts in sub-Saharan Africa, we demonstrate how our method quantifies critical underlying uncertainty, enabling these AA systems to meet the state-of-the-art demanded by donors and the WMO.}

\section{Introduction}

Increasingly, the international community is turning to forecast-based anticipatory action to mitigate the humanitarian impacts of disastrous weather events \citep{world_food_programme_10_2024}. This shift from a reactive to a proactive model of humanitarian assistance is made possible through scientific advances in medium-term forecasting, which allow skillful forecasts of seasonal total precipitation to be made as early as 3-6 months in advance. Seasonal forecasting is a comparatively young science when compared to weather forecasting and the policy application of seasonal forecasting is even younger -- the UN World Food Programme (WFP) has only been using seasonal forecasts for humanitarian action since the mid-2010s. 

This emerging field of practice demands new standards of consistency, robustness, and transparency, particularly when the human costs of inaction are high. Accordingly, the WFP has adopted the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) Guidelines for Seasonal Forecasting \citep{world_meteorological_organization_guidance_2020}, which mandate that forecasts be rooted in process-based modeling of hydrological conditions and use statistical methods that can accurately represent both observation- and model-based uncertainty in a probabilistic prediction.  

However, these guidelines do not mandate how to account for one very practical source of uncertainty: the uncertainty in thresholds for rare climate events due to the limited historical record. In practice, these thresholds are thus often estimated using samples of only several dozen, representing the number of years in a local observational record or over which seasonal forecast models have been run, and the true effective sample size is further reduced by the presence of low-frequency variability in the climate system and the resultant autocorrelation in precipitation signals. This type of uncertainty, hereafter called sampling uncertainty, matters because forecast-based anticipatory action decision rules are typically based on some rare event threshold. For example, drought-related humanitarian actions may be triggered only during the driest 20 percent of years as defined using the historical record. In practice, forecasts often fall very close to this threshold. Because decision makers have an intuitive understanding that these thresholds are uncertain, they must exercise discretion in determining whether or not to act in these situations. At the same time, they must ensure that the frequency of action is predictable enough that donors and governments can budget based on estimates of long-term expected program cost. 

While the sampling uncertainty in trigger threshold is currently handled in practice qualitatively, it can be quantified through the resampling of historical records. Accordingly, we present a bootstrap resampling-based method for estimating the probability of mis-triggering anticipatory action for rare events. This method was developed to meet a practical need expressed by WFP. We demonstrate the application of this method to operational seasonal forecasts used in WFP's drought anticipatory action projects and discuss how the incorporation of our threshold uncertainty methodology addresses crucial aspects of practical decision-making that conventional metrics of predictive skill alone cannot. We conclude with a discussion on how this methodology could be applied more broadly.

\section{Forecast-based anticipatory action}

\subsection{Institutional background of AA}

According to WFP's 2024 Anticipatory Action - A Decade in Review report, "WFP expanded its Anticipatory Action operations from covering 4.1 million people in 36 countries in 2023 to over 6.2 million people in 44 countries in 2024" \citep{world_food_programme_10_2024}. In 2024 alone, WFP's AA programs triggered over \$27 million USD in early action programming, reaching nearly 9 million people. WFP's AA programming includes forecasts of drought, flood and cyclones; this paper focuses on seasonal drought forecasting as a case study, but the practical problems of rare event forecast interpretation described herein are applicable across a variety of timescales and hazards.

As a member of the United Nations inter-agency Central Emergency Response Fund (CERF), WFP is subject to common standards for transparency, accuracy and reliability of its forecast-based AA programs. These standards are particularly crucial when negotiating the conditions under which AA donor funds will be disbursed - the so-called "anticipatory action plan" (AAP) - and determining the long-term expected cost of these programs. For seasonal forecasts of drought, the technical standards are primarily determined by the WMO Seasonal Forecasting Guidelines \citep{world_meteorological_organization_guidance_2020}. 

The primary concern of the WMO Guidelines is ensuring the seasonal forecasts are physically plausible, not overfit to limited data, and accurately represent the various sources of uncertainty in their probabilistic predictions. For drought forecasts, WFP predominately relies on the Climate Predictability Tool (CPT) forecasting system \citep{acharya_next_2021}, which was developed with the WMO guidelines in mind. 

However, while forecasting systems like CPT adhere to WMO's scientific best practices for accuracy, transparency and reproducibility, forecasts alone cannot determine the criteria for triggering anticipatory action, a crucial part of the AAP. To do so, stakeholders must exercise some judgment in determining a numeric threshold of forecast confidence for triggering action; the following section describes the typical procedure employed.

\subsection{Triggering humanitarian action using seasonal forecasts}

A core component of the Anticipatory Action Plan is the forecast “trigger”, i.e., the quantitative threshold of predicted rainfall below which a certain anticipatory action will be taken. The basic rationale behind the choice of triggers is that they should be transparent, consistently applied and easily related to the expected frequency with which action will be taken. A humanitarian agency planning budgets for AA programs should be able to look at the trigger rule associated with a specific action and know its long-term expected value based on historical analysis. 

Accordingly, the trigger rule used for WFP's AA programs is based on a consistent frequency of action for a given type of programming, across locations and times of year. For instance, in the Somali Region of Ethiopia, WFP's “Severe” level actions are associated with a 20\% frequency of action. This means that for a given location, for the forecast issued in a given month, one should expect "Severe" actions to trigger during the strongest 20\% of drought forecasts. To determine the numeric threshold associated with acting 20\% of the time, the decision-maker ranks all of the historical forecasts (“hindcasts”) available from the least likely prediction of drought to the most likely and computes the 20th percentile of this distribution.

This method is highly sensitive to the estimated percentile value used to set the trigger threshold. Even though forecast downscaling methods like CPT calibrate for observational and model uncertainty, the rare event threshold itself is subject to the inherent limitations of our comparatively short observational record, which begins in the 1980s for commonly used gridded precipitation products like CHIRPS \citep{funk_climate_2015} and GPCP \citep{huffman_improving_2009}. Consequently, decision-makers must exercise some judgment when a seasonal forecast is just above or just below the agreed-upon trigger threshold, as often happens. This is typically done through heuristic means such as adjusting the trigger threshold down by a few percentage points when observational indicators of a potential humanitarian crisis are present. Examples of such observational indicators include below-average vegetation conditions leading up to the season and assessments of food insecurity from the Integrated Phase Classification \citep{funk_recognizing_2019}. However, such heuristic approaches for determining when to trigger aid are inherently subjective, and risk becoming financially unsustainable if the choice to trigger is exercised too frequently. A more systematic way of assessing threshold sampling uncertainty is needed to meet the WFP's call for a robust, transparent decision support system. 

\subsection{Uncertainty due to the limited historical record}

The observational precipitation record is limited to fewer than 50 years in many regions with AA programs. Interannual and interdecadal autocorrelation in the climate patterns driving seasonal precipitation further reduce the effective sample size used to calculate percentile-based thresholds \citep{martinez_seasonal_2022}. This uncertainty in AA thresholds increases the risk that action is not triggered when it is needed or that programs become financially unsustainable due to excessive triggers. 

To address this problem, we need a statistical method to determine the \textit{range} of plausible threshold values. That is, we aim to construct confidence intervals for trigger thresholds such that forecasts falling within these intervals are statistically equivalent. Then, decision-makers can determine whether or not to exercise discretion in the decision to trigger action by assessing whether a given forecast value is above/below the threshold due to chance alone. Here, we present a flexible, resampling-based method for quantifying this uncertainty that can be easily adapted to existing operational AA frameworks.

\section{Bootstrapping to Quantify Sampling Uncertainty of Trigger Thresholds}

This section presents an uncertainty analysis to determine the sensitivity of a forecast-based anticipatory action protocol to the chosen trigger threshold. To do so, we repeatedly re-estimate the trigger threshold with resampled draws of the historical record. Repeating this procedure many times gives us an estimated distribution of how much the threshold might be expected to vary due to sampling error of a limited historical record. To account for correlation in time series due to modes of interannual climate variability such as ENSO, we implement block-boostrapping. As such, we run 1,000 iterations of the re-sampling process in which we draw a sample with replacement from the original data comprised of randomly sampled blocks of 5 consecutive years.

To illustrate this method, we first apply it to the observational record of precipitation from the Global Precipitation Climatology Project (GCPC) dataset \citep{huffman_improving_2009} over the period 1981-2025. \textbf{Figure 1} depicts, for each season, the variability in the 20th percentile threshold for seasonal total precipitation (a common choice for the anticipatory action trigger, as described above). We see that the degree of sampling uncertainty is proportionally large in much of the world, and varies across regions. Areas of greater uncertainty tend to be in regions that exhibit greater decadal variability in precipitation (\textbf{Figure A1}), although the degree of uncertainty may also be influenced by factors like missing data in the historical record. 

We note that this block-bootstrapping method makes the assumption that the historical precipitation record is stationary. That is, free from trends over time. This is a reasonable assumption for much of the world, particularly due to the high internal variability of seasonal precipitation. However, this method will provide biased estimates in the presence of substantial historical or projected trends. If a user is employing this method in regions with known trends, a non-stationary method that accounts for this underlying trend should be used.

\textbf{Figure 2} illustrates the same method applied to hindcast data from CFSv2 \citep{saha_ncep_2014}, initialized with a one-month lead time, instead of observed precipitation. The CFSv2 model is among those commonly used by WFP for downscaled seasonal forecasting. We see similar patterns of spatial variability in trigger uncertainty in both the forecast and observational datasets, although the absolute amount of uncertainty tends to be lower in the forecast dataset.

\section{Application to WFP assistance programs}

Finally, we illustrate how the results of our bootstrapping method could be applied to a common problem in forecast-based humanitarian decision-making. We focus on the case of the Somali region of Ethiopia, in which droughts often lead to large-scale risks to food security \citep{coughlan_de_perez_rain_2019}. GCMs have known limitations in modeling the agriculturally crucial March-April-May rains in this region \citep{schwarzwald_large-scale_2023, schwarzwald_understanding_2023}; as such, WFP is often faced with forecasts of drought in this region that are uncertain or ambiguous due to low calibrated model confidence. This leads to situations in which local decision-makers must exercise discretion in whether or not to trigger action on the basis of a borderline drought forecast, as detailed in \textbf{Section 2b}. 

The 2000 MAM season drought is a prime example of this issue - while many indicators on the ground pointed toward heightened risk of drought impact, GCM-based forecast methods would have failed to predict the full extent of this drought, leading to costly inaction \citep{funk_tailored_2023}. While in hindsight, the 2000 MAM season was a clear case in which the use of local expert discretion could have adverted a costly mistake, discretion cannot be exercised on a completely ad hoc basis if anticipatory action is to remain predictable, transparent and financially sustainable. Decision-makers need a way of quantifying the range of situations in which they might reasonably exercise discretion given an ambiguous seasonal outlook.  

Our bootstrapping method offers a practical solution to this problem. To illustrate how it might be applied, \textbf{Figure \ref{fig:casestudy}} depicts the cumulative distribution of MAM hindcasts from the CFS model (the black slope). A 20th percentile trigger threshold calculated on the full range of CFS hindcasts would result in a seasonal average of 1.5 mm/day; calculating the same threshold across block-bootstrap runs instead gives a plausible range of 0.1 mm above and below this value. 

\begin{figure}
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{2. figures/case_study_somalireg_MAM_2000_NCEP-CFSv2-ensmean.pdf}}
\caption{Case study of the 2000 MAM season in the Somali Region of Ethiopia. These figures show the predicted precipitation from the CFSv2 one-month lead time forecast for that season. The top row of maps show the predicted precipitation anomaly, its distance from the trigger threshold, and the range of uncertainty in that threshold over space. The bottom row of plots shows the areal average predicted precipitation over Somali Region as a time series and cumulative distribution. The bootstrapped range of trigger uncertainty is shown as the shaded area.}
\label{fig:casestudy}
\end{figure}

If the current forecast is outside of this sensitivity range, this indicates to the decision-maker that there is statistically robust evidence that the forecasted conditions are above or below the threshold. If the current forecast falls within the sensitivity range, this indicates that the decision-maker should interpret the trigger result for the current season with caution, as the appearance of trigger (un-)worthy conditions could be occurring just by chance. We can see that the 2000 MAM season forecast (the blue dot on the bottom row of \textbf{Figure 3}) was such a case, falling just above the trigger threshold. In this case, decision-makers faced with an uncertain outlook would have been \textit{ex ante} justified in exercising discretion in whether or not to trigger, as based on our statistical analysis. 

This principle of heightened caution when faced with an ambiguous forecast is informed not only by the scientific accuracy of precipitation prediction, but also by the human costs of failing to act. Since regional vegetation conditions leading into the 2000 MAM season were below average, and the previous OND season was poor, pastoral communities were at heightened risk of livestock loss going into the season \citep{mauerman_long-term_2023}. Accounting for these factors explicitly in the trigger rule - by allowing for greater uncertainty in the trigger decision if antecedent conditions indicate high humanitarian risk - is one way our bootstrapping analysis might be operationalized. 

Importantly, this bootstrapping method can only capture the uncertainty from variability on a temporal scale of similar or lower order than the length of the record used. If, for example, a major flood (such as MAM 1981 in southern Ethiopia, shown in \textbf{Figure A2}) occurs roughly every 50 years, but we only have 30 years of data with which to measure precipitation, our bootstrapping method will be unable to capture uncertainty in the threshold for events of that return period. However, since humanitarian anticipatory action tends to be based on relatively frequent return periods (e.g. the 1 out of 5-year drought), this limitation is less of a concern in our practical setting.  

\section{Conclusions and best practices}

Uncertainty due to the limited historical record of rare events is a significant issue for forecast-based anticipatory action, but is rarely accounted for when deciding whether to trigger AA efforts in a particular season. This uncertainty comes both from the limited length of the historical record (or the years for which hindcasts from seasonal forecast models are available) and from autocorrelation caused by variability in the climate system at different timescales, both of which limit the effective sample size over which AA thresholds are calculated. In this paper, we propose a methodology based on block-bootstrap resampling of this limited record to estimate the sampling uncertainty in AA thresholds. This methodology can be implemented easily, requires minimal assumptions, can be flexibly applied to any threshold, and accounts for interannual and decadal correlation in the historical record. The method is agnostic to the type of data used, and could accommodate more elaborate forecast-based decision rules so long as the historical data necessary to evaluate those rules is available. Through a case study based on the 2000 MAM rains in the Somali region in Ethiopia and illustrative of United Nations operational AA forecast procedures in sub-Saharan Africa, we demonstrate how our method provides a better quantification of critical underlying uncertainty, enabling these AA systems to meet the state-of-the-art demanded by donors and the WMO.

Balancing the need to provide effective humanitarian responses with the need to ensure the long-term financial solvency of a given AA program, which is based on an accurate calculation of the triggering rate, is a challenge when observational or modeling records are short. In practice, forecasts close to the trigger threshold invite judgment calls on whether to trigger AA responses, often informed by the presence of non-climate risk factors, such as existing levels of food insecurity. A better quantification of uncertainties in the trigger threshold, as proposed here, will allow for a more systemic treatment of these edge cases, by arming an operational forecaster with a way of judging whether the same forecast, when compared to an observational record of a different length or a sequence of years that were slightly different within plausible internal variability, would have instead triggered.
